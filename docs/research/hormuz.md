# The Hormuz Supply-Chain Crisis: What Comes Next

*Last updated: April 2026*

The closure of the Strait of Hormuz is widely covered as an oil and gas shock. But Hormuz is also a logistics chokepoint for container shipping, dry bulk, food imports, and fertiliser — and the supply-chain consequences will outlast the closure itself.

Using [DisruptSC](https://disrupt-sc.io), a spatial agent-based model that routes cargo through ports, pipelines, roads, railways, and maritime links and then tracks how disruptions cascade through supply chains into the real economy, I have been running scenarios on the Hormuz crisis. This page summarises the key findings.

---

## The closure is not only an energy shock

Before the closure, roughly **25% of vessels transiting Hormuz were container and bulk carriers**, not oil or gas tankers. Gulf Cooperation Council states import over **80% of their calories** through the Strait. The disruption extends across manufactured goods, food, fertiliser, and petrochemical inputs.

## Bypass capacity is limited — and already strained

Saudi Arabia and the UAE have bypass infrastructure (the East–West pipeline to the Red Sea, the Habshan–Fujairah pipeline to the open-sea side). But these corridors were not designed to absorb the full volume of Gulf trade. In March 2026, even with bypasses operational, Gulf oil exports were roughly halved. For container and bulk cargo, rerouting often means crossing borders, using congested ports, and accepting longer, costlier delivery.

## The system is approaching a critical threshold

In the first weeks, inventories and strategic stocks absorbed much of the shock. But as the crisis enters its seventh week, those buffers are running thin. The model shows that once intermediate stocks are depleted, disruptions begin to compound: one affected sector pulls others down, and the economic damage accelerates. This threshold effect means the crisis could deepen sharply even without further military escalation.

## Reopening will not end the disruption

COVID-19 demonstrated that supply chains do not simply switch back on. Inventories have been depleted, firms are trying to catch up unevenly, transport systems remain under strain, and coordination across global networks takes time to restore. The model points to a recovery phase that is messy, stop-and-start, and potentially lasting months — with continued pressure on food prices, manufacturing output, and logistics costs.

## Who is most exposed

- **Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar**: fully dependent on Hormuz for seaborne access; no bypass routes
- **India**: low oil and gas reserves; gas-dependent industries have already curtailed production
- **Gulf food importers**: price effects persist even where strategic food stocks prevent outright shortages
- **South and Southeast Asia**: cascading effects through manufacturing supply chains

## About this analysis

This work uses [DisruptSC](https://github.com/ccolon/disrupt-sc), an open-source spatial agent-based model that combines transport networks, supply-chain linkages, and macroeconomic accounts. It has been applied to national and regional analyses in Tanzania, Cambodia, Ecuador, Central Asia, and at the global scale, and published in [*Nature Sustainability*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41893-020-00649-4).

The Hormuz analysis is ongoing research and has not yet been peer-reviewed. Critical feedback is welcome.
